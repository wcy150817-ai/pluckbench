The Mathematical Formulas Behind BPM, Milliseconds (ms), and LFO Hertz (Hz)

Every digital audio workstation (Pro Tools, Logic Pro, Ableton Live, Reaper, Cubase), hardware tape echo, and analog pedalboard operates on the inverse relationship between musical tempo (measured in quarter-note Beats Per Minute) and physical time (measured in milliseconds or cycles per second). Because one standard clock minute contains 60 seconds—or 60,000 milliseconds—you can derive any note value directly from a single fundamental equation:

T1/4 Note (ms) = 60,000 / BPM   |   fLFO (Hz) = 1,000 / T (ms) = BPM / 60

Once the quarter-note pulse ($T_{1/4}$) is known, every standard binary subdivision, dotted rhythmic value, and triplet grouping follows exact mathematical multipliers:

  • Straight Binary Subdivisions: Each successive note division halves the millisecond duration and doubles the LFO frequency. A half note ($1/2$) is $2 \times T_{1/4}$ ($120,000 / \text{BPM}$); an eighth note ($1/8$) is $0.5 \times T_{1/4}$ ($30,000 / \text{BPM}$); a sixteenth note ($1/16$) is $0.25 \times T_{1/4}$ ($15,000 / \text{BPM}$); a thirty-second note ($1/32$) is $0.125 \times T_{1/4}$ ($7,500 / \text{BPM}$); and a sixty-fourth note ($1/64$) is $0.0625 \times T_{1/4}$ ($3,750 / \text{BPM}$).
  • Dotted Subdivisions ($\times 1.5$ Multiplier): In musical notation, placing a dot after a note lengthens its duration by 50% ($1 + 0.5 = 1.5\times$). Consequently, a Dotted Quarter Note equals $1.5 \times (60,000 / \text{BPM}) = 90,000 / \text{BPM}$, and the famous Dotted Eighth Note equals $1.5 \times (30,000 / \text{BPM}) = 45,000 / \text{BPM}$ (exactly three-quarters of a quarter beat, or three sixteenth notes).
  • Triplet Subdivisions ($\times 2/3 \approx 0.6667$ Multiplier): A triplet fits three evenly spaced notes into the time span normally occupied by two notes of the same denomination. Therefore, multiply the straight note duration by $2/3$ ($0.6667$). An Eighth-Note Triplet divides a single quarter beat into three equal parts: $(30,000 / \text{BPM}) \times (2/3) = 20,000 / \text{BPM}$.

How to Read the Chart Without Making Common Mistakes

  • Check which note value your plugin calls "1". Some delay plugins label a quarter note "1/4" while hardware pedals with a tap-tempo switch treat each tap as a quarter note by default. If your pedal has a subdivision switch, the tap still sets the quarter-note value and the switch applies the multiplier for you.
  • Half-time and double-time grooves change the feel, not the math. A trap beat at 140 BPM that "feels like 70" still uses the 140 BPM row if your DAW project is set to 140. Use the 70 BPM row only if the project tempo itself is 70.
  • Pre-delay is a starting point. The 1/64-note column gives a tempo-locked reverb pre-delay that keeps vocals clear; many engineers then adjust by ear between roughly 10 and 60 ms. Treat the number as a reference, not a rule.
  • LFO rates scale linearly. The Hz column is the rate for one LFO cycle per quarter note. Double it for one cycle per eighth note, halve it for one cycle per half note.
  • Fractional tempos. Film and game cues sometimes run at tempos such as 117.5 BPM. The chart lists whole numbers only, so type the exact tempo into the calculator above for those cases.

Worked Example: Setting a Dotted-Eighth Guitar Delay at 72 BPM

Suppose a slow worship ballad runs at 72 BPM and you want the classic dotted-eighth echo. The quarter note is 60,000 / 72 = 833.3 ms. A dotted eighth is three-quarters of that: 833.3 × 0.75 = 625.0 ms. Set the delay time to 625 ms, keep the feedback low (two or three audible repeats), and play straight eighth notes; the repeats land on the sixteenth-note gaps between your picked notes. If the echo feels too busy at that slow tempo, try the straight quarter note (833 ms) instead, which leaves more space between phrases. For the vocal reverb on the same song, the 1/64-note pre-delay is 833.3 / 16 = 52.1 ms, a reasonable value to start from before adjusting by ear.

How Audio Engineers & Guitarists Use Tempo-Synced Milliseconds in Production

1. The Dotted Eighth-Note Guitar Delay Trick (3/16 Subdivision)

Popularized by guitarists such as The Edge (U2), David Gilmour (Pink Floyd), and modern worship and post-rock players, setting a delay pedal to a dotted eighth note ($45,000 / \text{BPM}$) while picking steady eighth notes creates an interlocking rhythmic cascade. Because the first delay repeat arrives exactly 3/16ths of a beat after the dry pick attack, every echo lands squarely on the off-beat sixteenth notes between your picked strokes, generating a fast sixteenth-note rhythmic ostinato from a simple eighth-note right-hand pattern.

2. Haas Effect Doubling vs Rockabilly Slapback Echo (15 ms to 140 ms)

Psychoacoustic research by Helmut Haas demonstrates that when a delayed copy of a sound reaches the human ear within 1 ms to 35 ms of the direct sound, the brain fuses the two arrivals into a single perceived image rather than hearing a discrete echo—a phenomenon known as the Haas Fusion Window. Panning a dry electric guitar left and a 18 ms to 28 ms (roughly a 1/64 or 1/128 note) delayed duplicate right creates wide stereo thickening. Once delay time crosses 65 ms to 135 ms (typically a 1/32 or 1/16 note at medium tempos), the ear perceives a distinct, tight single bounce—the classic 1950s Sun Studio slapback echo used on lead vocals and country telecaster solos.

3. Syncing Reverb Pre-Delay & RT60 Decay Tail to the Bar Grid

Placing a lush plate or hall reverb directly on a lead vocal with 0 ms of pre-delay smears the initial consonants ("t", "k", "p", "s") and pushes the singer backward into the virtual room. By setting Reverb Pre-Delay to a tempo-synced 1/64 note (for example, 31.3 ms at 120 BPM) or 1/32 note (62.5 ms at 120 BPM), you open a brief acoustic gap between the dry vocal front and the onset of early reflections. To prevent the reverb tail from muddying the next chord change, subtract your Pre-Delay from a full 1-bar or 1/2-bar duration:

RT60 Decay Time (ms) = T1 Bar (ms) − Pre-Delay (ms)

4. Bus Compressor Release Times & LFO Modulation Rates

On a stereo mix bus or drum parallel compressor (such as an SSL G-Bus or VCA compressor), setting the Release Time to a 1/16 note or 1/8 note millisecond value allows gain reduction to recover completely just before the next kick or snare hit arrives, making the compressor "breathe" in rhythm with the song. Similarly, when programming analog chorus, phaser, rotary speaker, or tremolo units calibrated in Hertz, setting the LFO rate to the 1/4 note ($\text{BPM} / 60$) or Dotted 1/8 note ($\text{BPM} / 45$) ensures every filter sweep peaks in lockstep with the bar lines.

Frequently Asked Questions: BPM to Milliseconds & LFO Hz

How many milliseconds is 72 BPM?

At 72 BPM one quarter note lasts 60,000 / 72 = 833.3 milliseconds. An eighth note is 416.7 ms, a dotted eighth is 625.0 ms, an eighth-note triplet is 277.8 ms, a sixteenth note is 208.3 ms, and one bar of 4/4 lasts 3,333 ms.

What is the mathematical formula to convert BPM to milliseconds (ms)?

Because one minute contains 60,000 milliseconds, the duration of one quarter-note beat in milliseconds is 60,000 divided by the tempo in BPM. For example, at 120 BPM, one quarter note equals 60,000 / 120 = 500 milliseconds, an eighth note equals 250 ms, and a sixteenth note equals 125 ms.

How do you calculate dotted eighth-note delay time in milliseconds?

A dot adds half of a note's value to itself (multiplying duration by 1.5). Since a standard eighth note is half of a quarter note (30,000 / BPM), a dotted eighth note equals 45,000 / BPM, or 0.75 times the quarter-note duration. At 120 BPM, a dotted eighth delay is 375 milliseconds.

How do you convert BPM to Hertz (Hz) for synthesizer LFOs, phasers, and tremolo pedals?

Frequency in Hertz (cycles per second) is the reciprocal of period in seconds: Hz = 1,000 / ms, or equivalently BPM / 60 for a quarter-note cycle. At 120 BPM, a quarter-note LFO runs at 2.00 Hz, an eighth-note LFO runs at 4.00 Hz, and a dotted-eighth LFO runs at 2.67 Hz.

What is the ideal reverb pre-delay time in milliseconds for vocals and snare drums?

Audio engineers typically sync reverb pre-delay to a 1/64 note (3,750 / BPM) or 1/32 note (7,500 / BPM) so the dry vocal consonant or snare transient clears the Haas fusion window (15 to 35 ms) before the wet reverb tail blooms in rhythm with the track.
